A practical guide for families

Protect the Men We Love from the Manosphere

How to recognize the warning signs, keep the conversation open, and help the men in our lives build confidence without anger, contempt or manufactured fear.

The manosphere is not just a collection of online arguments about women and masculinity. It is a loose ecosystem of influencers, podcasts and online communities that can package resentment as insight, dominance as strength, and suspicion as common sense. Some content taps into real feelings—loneliness, insecurity, economic pressure or a desire for purpose—and then offers a narrow answer: become harder, distrust others, and treat life as a contest for power.

Families cannot control every video that appears on someone’s screen. But they can notice changes, maintain relationships and help the people they love develop better ways to judge what they are being shown.

The goal is not to police what men think. It is to make sure they have the confidence, information and real-world connections to think for themselves.

First, recognize the warning signs

One phrase or political opinion does not tell you that someone has been drawn into the manosphere. Look instead for a pattern: a sudden shift in language, attitudes and what occupies their attention.

1. A new vocabularySuddenly using terms such as “simp,” “red-pilled,” “alpha,” “beta,” “soy boy” or “high-value man” as labels for people.
2. Growing contempt toward womenMaking sweeping claims about women, dismissing their views, or describing relationships as battles for control.
3. Compassion becomes weaknessMocking kindness, empathy, emotional openness or men who show affection and care.
4. Fixation on force and national humiliationBecoming preoccupied with weapons, military dominance, nuclear arms or claims that the country is constantly on the brink of attack.
5. Broad distrust of public institutionsDismissing education, healthcare, infrastructure or social supports as worthless, while treating force and military power as the answer to almost every problem.
6. An increasingly hostile feedSpending more time with videos built around outrage, humiliation, enemies, conspiracies or a constant sense of emergency.
Keep perspective: Interest in defence, criticism of government, or using an internet slang term is not proof of radicalization. The concern is a sustained pattern of contempt, fear, rigid thinking and withdrawal from more balanced sources and relationships.

Three things families can do

1

Keep the relationship open. Challenge ideas, not the person.

Ridicule and angry confrontations can push someone deeper into an online community that promises acceptance. Start with curiosity. Ask what he watches, what he finds persuasive and how a particular video made him feel. Listen long enough to understand the appeal before challenging the claim.

  • Ask: “Where did you hear that?” and “What evidence would change your mind?”
  • Separate the person from the claim: “I care about you. I’m not convinced by that argument.”
  • Check dramatic claims together using multiple credible sources. Be especially careful with urgent claims about imminent attacks, national collapse or secret plots.
  • Do not turn every family interaction into a debate. Keep sharing meals, humour, interests and ordinary time together.
Try saying“I can see this matters to you. Help me understand what convinced you. Can we look at the original source together?”
2

Help him take control of his information environment.

Social-media feeds are not neutral windows onto the world. Recommendation systems select what to show based on signals such as viewing and engagement. Repeatedly watching provocative material can lead to more of it appearing. That does not mean every platform, account or user follows the same path—but it does mean a feed can become narrower and more extreme than the person intended.

  • Talk openly about how recommendation systems work. A video appearing repeatedly does not make its claims true or widely accepted.
  • Use platform controls such as “Not interested,” unfollow, mute and block. Review watch history and recommendation settings where available.
  • Deliberately diversify the feed: follow credible journalism, science, history, thoughtful male role models, educators and creators with different perspectives.
  • Pause before sharing emotionally charged videos. Find the full interview, original statement, data or document—not just a clipped reaction.
  • Set boundaries around endless short-video scrolling, especially when it is replacing sleep, study, work or relationships.
Try saying“Your feed is learning what keeps you watching. Let's see whether you can change what it learns about you.”
3

Reconnect him with purpose, competence and real people.

Online influencers can offer a ready-made identity: a community, an enemy and a simple formula for becoming powerful. A stronger answer is not another influencer. It is a fuller life in which a man can experience belonging, achievement and contribution without needing to look down on anyone.

  • Encourage friendships and activities that involve cooperation: team sports, music, clubs, mentoring or community projects.
  • Make room for meaningful responsibilities—helping family, volunteering, learning a skill or contributing to a local cause.
  • Introduce men who model strength through patience, integrity, courage, curiosity and care—not just wealth, aggression or dominance.
  • Talk about masculinity in positive terms: being dependable, protecting others without controlling them, admitting mistakes, asking for help and treating people with dignity.
  • If anger, isolation, anxiety or compulsive online behaviour is seriously affecting daily life, encourage support from a qualified counsellor or mental-health professional without shaming him.
Try saying“Being strong isn't about having someone to hate. It's about having something worthwhile to build and people you can count on.”

Why the algorithm deserves attention

A real-world experiment—not just a theory

In its April 2024 report Recommending Toxicity, Dublin City University's Anti-Bullying Centre created ten experimental teenage-boy profiles on TikTok and YouTube Shorts. Some profiles watched ordinary male-interest content such as sport, gaming and gym videos; others showed interest in manosphere material.

26 minManosphere content appeared within the first 26 minutes across the experimental accounts.
76% / 78%By the final round—after about 400 videos—the share of recommended content classified by the researchers as toxic was 76% on TikTok and 78% on YouTube Shorts.
The study involved a small number of controlled experimental accounts, not a representative sample of all users. Its results show what happened in that experiment; they do not prove that every boy will receive the same recommendations or that algorithms alone cause a person’s beliefs.

The experiment examined TikTok and YouTube Shorts, not Facebook. Facebook and Instagram also use recommendation systems, including Reels, but the specific findings above should not be presented as if they measured Facebook. More independent research is needed to make platform-by-platform comparisons.

Don't let fear make every decision

One particularly concerning pattern is the use of alarming claims about foreign threats, invasion or national humiliation to create a permanent sense of emergency. A country’s security deserves serious, evidence-based debate. But a frightening video is not a defence assessment, and a call for ever-greater military power should not end discussion about healthcare, education, housing, jobs or infrastructure.

Teach the men in your family to ask: What is the evidence? Who is making this claim? What is being left out? What other ways could the problem be addressed? The point is not to tell them what conclusion to reach. It is to help them resist being rushed into one by fear.

We don't protect men by teaching them to fear the world.
We protect them by helping them understand it, take part in it, and make it better.
